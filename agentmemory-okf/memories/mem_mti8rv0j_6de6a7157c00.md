### What evidence is there that Jesus actually existed?

Historians can’t “prove” an individual from antiquity the way a modern court might, but they *can* ask whether the best explanation of the sources is that a real person stood behind the later traditions. On that question, the mainstream view in historical scholarship is that Jesus of Nazareth was a real 1st‑century Jewish teacher who was crucified under Pontius Pilate.

What follows is the core evidence base and how historians weigh it.

### The strongest evidence: early Christian sources (especially Paul)

#### 1) Paul’s letters (c. 50s CE)
These are the earliest surviving Christian texts—written within ~20–30 years of Jesus’ death. Paul is not writing a biography, but he treats Jesus as a recent historical person and references:

- Jesus being **crucified**
- Jesus being **Jewish** (“born under the law”)
- Jesus having **disciples**
- Jesus having a **brother named James** (Paul mentions meeting “James, the Lord’s brother”)
- Jesus having a **last

supper** tradition (Paul passes on a tradition about the night Jesus was betrayed)

Why this matters: Paul personally knew (or at least interacted with) key figures in the Jerusalem movement (e.g., Peter/Cephas and James). That proximity is a major reason historians regard Jesus’ existence as the simplest explanation.

#### 2) The Gospels (Mark ~70 CE; Matthew/Luke ~80–90; John ~90–100)
The Gospels are later than Paul and contain theology and legendary development, so historians treat them critically. Still, they preserve multiple independent streams of tradition (not all stories are independent, but not all are the same either), and they center on a figure placed in a fairly specific time and place (Galilee/Judea) ending in execution by Roman authority.

Key point: even when you discount miracles and theological claims, it’s common historically that movements form around a founder and later elaborate the story—without requiring that the founder be invented from scratch.

### Non-Christian sources that

mention Jesus (or his execution)

These are not as early as Paul, but they’re important because they come from outside the Christian movement.

#### 3) Tacitus (early 2nd century CE)
A Roman historian who mentions that “Christus” was executed under **Pontius Pilate** during the reign of **Tiberius**, and that the movement spread afterward. Historians generally value this because Tacitus is hostile to Christians and is not trying to promote them.

#### 4) Josephus (late 1st century CE)
A Jewish historian whose works contain references to Jesus:
- A brief reference connected to **James**, described as the brother of Jesus “who is called Christ.” This passage is widely regarded as substantially authentic.
- Another longer passage (the *Testimonium Flavianum*) is disputed because later Christian scribes likely altered it; many scholars think it contains a genuine core with later embellishment.

#### 5) Other possible references (less direct / more debated)
- **Pliny the Younger**: describes Christians worshiping

Christ, but doesn’t really add biographical detail.
- **Suetonius**: mentions disturbances involving “Chrestus,” which is ambiguous.
- **Mara bar Serapion**: refers to a “wise king” executed by Jews (interpretation debated).

### What historians say is most likely true (minimal “historical core”)

Across the sources, the most widely accepted minimal claims are:

- Jesus was a **Jewish teacher/preacher** in 1st‑century Palestine.
- He was **associated with a movement** that continued after his death.
- He was **crucified** under Roman authority, commonly connected with **Pilate**.

These are “low bar” claims that don’t depend on accepting miracles
