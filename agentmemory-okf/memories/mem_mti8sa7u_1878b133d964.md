From this photo alone, I can’t identify the species with high confidence—this looks like a **light-colored, tight/straight-grained surface** that could be:

- **Bamboo (laminated/strand-woven)** — often shows very fine, linear “fiber-like” grain and a fairly uniform pale tone.
- **Maple or birch (common in furniture/plywood tops)** — usually light and relatively uniform, with subtle grain.
- **Rubberwood** (also common in inexpensive furniture) — pale, fairly even grain.

What makes it hard here is that the image doesn’t show the **end grain** or **a wide enough area** to catch telltale features (pores/rays/figure), and it could also be a **veneer** over a different core.

#### Quick checks that will narrow it down fast
1. **Take a photo of the end grain** (the cut edge of the board). This is the #1 identifier.
2. **Look for pores**:
   - If you see tiny holes/pores (especially in lines), it’s likely a hardwood like **oak/ash**.
   - If it looks very smooth/tight with no visible pores, **maple/birch/bamboo**

become more likely.
3. **Check for bamboo clues**: laminated bamboo often shows “strip” patterns and occasional node-like features (depending on how it’s made).
4. **Is it veneer?** If the edge shows a thin “skin” over a different-looking interior, it’s veneered—then the top grain can be misleading.

If you upload **(a)** a close-up of the **edge/end grain**, and **(b)** a wider shot of the surface in better light, I can give you a much more confident ID (or at least narrow it to 1–2 likely woods).

### 🤖 Assistant

This second photo changes things—the end grain makes it much clearer. This is very likely **Red Oak** or **White Oak** (with Red Oak being the most common for standard lumber store boards).

#### Why it's likely Oak:
*   **Ring-Porous Structure**: Notice the very distinct horizontal "bands" or layers in that end grain. You can see large, open pores in the earlywood (the wider, lighter bands), which is the hallmark of the oak family.
*   **Medullary Rays**: Look closely at the top surface in both photos. You can see those distinct, darker "flecking" or dash-like lines. In oak, these are medullary rays that run perpendicular to the growth rings.
*   **The Grain Pattern**: The "cathedral" arches in your first photo are classic for flat-sawn oak.

#### How to tell if it's Red vs. White Oak:
*   **The "Straw" Test**: If you can blow bubbles through a short piece of it into a glass of water, it's **Red Oak**. Red oak pores are open like tiny straws. White oak pores are plugged with a substance called tyloses,

which makes it waterproof (why it's used for whiskey barrels).
*   **Color**: Red oak usually has a slight pinkish or salmon hue when freshly cut, while white oak tends to be more tan, olive, or "wheat" colored. Based on the light tan/grayish tone in your photos, it could be White Oak, but lighting and sanding dust can make Red Oak look very similar.

#### One other possibility:
If the wood feels surprisingly light/soft and has a slightly "fuzzy" texture when sanded, it could be **Ash**. Ash looks very similar to oak in grain but lacks the prominent medullary rays (the little "flecks") that oak has. Looking at your first photo, those flecks are visible enough that I'd bet on **Oak**.
