In Texas, what are your legal recourses if someone refuses to pay you for labor done?

### 🤖 Assistant — 2025-08-02T13:04:31Z

In Texas, you have several legal options if someone refuses to pay you for labor performed:

**Texas Payday Law**
Texas has strong wage protection laws that require employers to pay wages when due. If you're an employee, your employer must pay wages by the next scheduled payday after the work period ends. Violations can result in penalties for the employer.

**Small Claims Court**
For amounts up to $20,000, you can file in small claims court (Justice of the Peace court). This is typically the most cost-effective option for most wage disputes. The process is designed to be accessible without an attorney.

**District or County Court**
For larger amounts, you can file in district or county court. While more formal and potentially requiring legal representation, these courts can handle unlimited claim amounts.

**Mechanic's Lien (for certain types of work)**
If you performed construction, repair, or improvement work on real property, you may be able to file a mechanic's lien against the property. This gives you a security interest in the property until payment is made.

**Texas Workforce Commission**
If you're an employee (not an independent contractor), you can file a wage claim with the Texas Workforce Commission. They can investigate and help recover unpaid wages at no cost to you.

**Before Filing Legal Action**
Document everything - contracts, work performed, communications about payment, and proof of completion. Send a formal demand letter requesting payment with a reasonable deadline.

The specific best option depends on your employment status, the amount owed, and the type of work performed. For significant amounts or complex situations, consulting with an employment or collections attorney would be advisable.

What happens after you win a judgement in small claims court or a monetary amount?

### 🤖 Assistant — 2025-08-02T13:06:17Z

After winning a monetary judgment in Texas, the court doesn't automatically collect the money for you - you'll need to take additional steps to actually get paid:

**Immediate Steps After Judgment**
The court will issue a written judgment stating the amount owed. The debtor typically has a grace period (usually 30 days) to pay voluntarily before you can begin enforcement actions.

**If They Don't Pay Voluntarily**
You become a "judgment creditor" and can use various collection methods:

**Wage Garnishment**
You can garnish the debtor's wages, but Texas has strict limits. Only disposable earnings above 30 times the federal minimum wage per week can be garnished, and only for certain types of debts.

**Bank Account Levy**
You can freeze and seize money from the debtor's bank accounts, though some funds (like certain government benefits) are protected.

**Property Seizure**
The sheriff can seize and sell non-exempt personal property to satisfy the judgment. However, Texas has generous homestead and personal property exemptions.

**Asset Discovery**
You can require the debtor to appear in court and disclose their assets, income, and financial information under oath.

**Lien on Real Property**
You can place a lien on any real estate the debtor owns in the county where the judgment was entered.

**Challenges in Collection**
Texas is very debtor-friendly with extensive exemptions protecting primary residences, vehicles, tools of trade, and other essential property. Some debtors may be "judgment proof" if they have no attachable assets or income.
