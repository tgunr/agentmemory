### 👤 User

Will a 64 W diode laser cut through clear glass onto stock? I'm trying to use the laser to melt powder coating paint and the fan keeps blowing the paint out.

---

### 🤖 Assistant

**No, a 64 W diode laser will not cut through clear glass** (or effectively interact with it in a meaningful way for cutting or melting material behind it).

Diode lasers (typically 450 nm blue light) pass straight through transparent materials like clear glass or clear acrylic because the material does not absorb that wavelength. The light transmits through without generating enough heat to cut, melt, or engrave the glass itself. Multiple sources confirm this limitation:

- Diode lasers cannot cut glass, clear acrylic, stone, ceramic, or metals.
- One workaround sometimes mentioned for *engraving* on glass is placing an opaque layer (like black paint or paper) *behind* the glass so the laser heats that layer and marks the glass surface indirectly—but this does not allow cutting through the glass or affecting material on the other side.

Even at 64 W (a relatively high power for a diode laser), the fundamental physics of wavelength absorption prevents it from working on clear glass. CO₂ lasers (far-infrared

wavelength) are the type that can handle clear glass and acrylic effectively.

### Your Powder Coating Issue
The fan (likely the air assist or exhaust fan) blowing away the loose powder coating paint is a very common problem. Powder coatings are fine, loose particles that get easily displaced by any airflow before the laser can heat and melt/fuse them properly.

**Quick suggestions to try:**
- Turn off or greatly reduce the air assist (many machines allow this per layer or job). Air assist is great for cutting wood/acrylic to clear smoke/debris, but it works against you with loose powders.
- Apply the powder differently: Use a light binder, spray adhesive, or even a thin layer of clear tape/masking to hold the powder in place temporarily.
- Lower the fan speed or use a more enclosed setup with minimal airflow over the work area.
- Test with very low power + slow speed + multiple passes to melt the powder without disturbing it.
- Consider a different approach altogether (e.g., pre-applying the powder coating

via traditional methods and then using the laser only for selective removal or marking).

If you're trying to laser *through* the glass onto the powder-coated stock below, the glass will block the beam entirely.

Would you like recommendations for alternative methods, settings ideas, or safer ways to handle powder coating with a diode laser?

### Will the laser work through the glass?
**Yes**, in principle. Clear glass is transparent to the ~450 nm blue wavelength that diode lasers use, so the beam should pass through the glass with relatively little absorption and reach the powder coating below. The glass itself should remain largely unaffected (no cutting or significant engraving on the glass).

The powder coating (being opaque and typically dark-colored) should absorb the laser energy and heat up/melt, just as it would without the glass.

### Important caveats and practical considerations
- **Focus adjustment is critical**: The laser must be focused on the *powder coating surface*, not on top of the glass. Measure the thickness of your glass plate and
