### 👤 User

I have a shop that is 30 ft by 30 ft, uses 6 in by 6 in studs spaced 10 ft apart. The inside has been sprayed with foam for insulation, and I desire to put some kind of interior wall. I'm thinking I can use some lap boards. The question is, can I use what kind of lap board to span the 10 ft between studs?

---

### 🤖 Assistant

That is quite a long span for standard lap boards! In typical construction, studs are spaced 16 or 24 inches apart. 

A 10-foot span without any intermediate support is very wide for common interior siding or lap boards. Most standard wood lap boards (like 1/2" or 3/4" cedar or pine) are not designed to be structural or to bridge a gap that large without sagging or bowing significantly over time, especially if they are subjected to any weight or humidity changes.

Here are some details on what you would need to consider for a project like this:

### The Challenge of a 10-Foot Span
Most lap siding is designed to be nailed into studs every 16 to 24 inches. Over a 10-foot (120-inch) span, the board has to support its own weight without twisting or sagging. 
* **Deflection:** A standard 3/4" thick board will likely sag in the middle over a 10-foot span.
* **Impact Resistance:** A wall with a 10-foot unsupported span would feel very "flimsy" or "bouncy" if someone leaned against it or if equipment bumped into

it.

### Possible Solutions and Materials
If you are set on using the 10-foot spacing of your main 6x6 posts, you have a few options to make this work:

#### 1. Horizontal Girts (The Professional Way)
Because your shop has foam insulation, you likely have a smooth but non-structural surface between the posts. The most common way to finish a pole-barn style building like this is to install **horizontal girts** (also called "purlins" when used on roofs). 
* You can nail 2x4s horizontally across the face of the foam/studs, spaced every 2 feet vertically. 
* This provides a solid "ladder" of wood that you can then nail any kind of lap board to. 
* It also protects the foam and creates a small air gap if needed.

#### 2. Thick Tongue and Groove (T&G)
If you do not want to add horizontal girts, you would need a much thicker product than standard lap board. 
* **2x6 Tongue and Groove:** Sometimes called "car siding" or "decking," this is actual 1.5-inch thick lumber. It is much more capable of bridging long spans,

though 10 feet is still pushing the limits for a wall that won't flex.
* **Rough-Cut Lumber:** If you have access to a local sawmill, 1-inch (true 4/4) or 5/4-inch thick boards would be significantly stiffer than store-bought 1/2" lap siding.

#### 3. Metal Siding
In many shops with 10-foot post spacing, interior "liner panels" made of steel are used. These are designed to span long distances between girts and are very durable for a shop environment.

### Recommendations
To get a professional result that doesn't sag or warp:
* **Add 2x4 blocking:** Even if you just add one or two vertical "floating" 2x4s between the 6x6 posts (attaching them to the floor plate and top plate), it would reduce your span to 5 feet or 3.3 feet, making standard lap boards much more viable.
* **Use a thicker board:** If you skip the extra framing, avoid thin 1/2" lap siding. Look for 3/4" or even 1" thick material.
* **Check Moisture:** Ensure the wood is well-seasoned. If you put up "green" or wet wood over a 10-foot span, it

will almost certainly warp as it dries.

If you can share a bit more about the look you're going for (rustic, clean, industrial), I can give you more specific material suggestions!
