Here’s a breakdown of the usual approaches:

### 🔩 Magnet Hole Placement
- **Bottom only:**  
  - Simplest and most common.  
  - Magnets go on the underside of the baseplate so it grips onto a metal surface (steel plate, tool chest, shelving, etc.).  
  - Keeps the top side clean and compatible with bins.  
  - Great if you want the baseplate itself to stay put.

- **Top only:**  
  - Less common.  
  - Magnets face upward, often intended to hold bins directly (if the bins have steel washers or magnets in their bottoms).  
  - Can interfere with smooth bin placement if not flush.  

- **Bottom *and* top:**  
  - For heavy-duty setups or swappable plates.  
  - Magnets below keep the base secured to a work surface.  
  - Magnets above can help lock bins more firmly in place, especially in mobile setups (tool carts, vertical storage, vans).  
  -

Trickier to design since top magnets must sit flush and not disrupt bin alignment.

### ⚙️ Design Notes
- Gridfinity relies mostly on the **grid + alignment lips** for stability, and magnets are an *optional helper*.  
- If your baseplate will always sit in one spot (desktop, wall-mounted sheet metal), bottom holes are usually enough.  
- If you’re building **portable / transport setups**, top + bin magnets can prevent rattling or bins falling out.  

👉 Quick rule of thumb:  
- **Stationary baseplate** → magnets on bottom.  
- **Portable or moving system** → consider both top + bottom.

### 🤖 Assistant

To specify magnet holes on the bottom of a Gridfinity baseplate, you'd typically design them as **recesses or pockets** on the underside of the plate. This allows the magnets to be inserted and sit flush with or slightly below the bottom surface.

Here's how you'd generally approach it in a CAD program or when describing the design:

### 📐 Design Specifications for Bottom Magnet Holes

1.  **Location:**
    *   The magnet holes should be centered within each Gridfinity unit cell on the *bottom* face of the baseplate.
    *   For a standard 1x1 Gridfinity unit, there's usually one magnet hole. For larger baseplates, you'd repeat this pattern across the entire bottom surface.

2.  **Dimensions (Diameter/Side Length):**
    *   The hole's diameter (for cylindrical magnets) or side length (for square magnets) should match the magnet's dimensions precisely for a snug fit.
    *   Common magnet sizes for Gridfinity are 6x2mm or 6x3mm (diameter x thickness). So, a 6mm diameter hole is typical.

3.  **Depth:**
    *

The depth of the hole should be equal to or slightly less than the thickness of the magnet.
    *   If the hole is *exactly* the magnet's thickness, the magnet will sit flush.
    *   If the hole is *slightly shallower* (e.g., 1.8mm deep for a 2mm thick magnet), the magnet will protrude slightly, which can improve magnetic contact with an uneven surface.
    *   If the hole is *deeper*, the magnet will be recessed, reducing its effective pull.

4.  **Tolerance:**
    *   A small tolerance (e.g., 0.1mm larger than the magnet) can be added to the hole's diameter to account for manufacturing variations and make magnet insertion easier. However, for a tight press-fit, you might design it to be exactly the magnet's size or even slightly smaller.
