### 👤 User

How much information can be stored in 1 mg of DNA?

---

### 🤖 Assistant

Based on current scientific estimates, 1 mg of DNA can store an immense amount of data, though the exact figures vary by research study due to different encoding efficiencies and theoretical limits.

Because scientists typically measure DNA storage capacity in grams, you can calculate the capacity for 1 mg by dividing the gram-based estimates by 1,000. Here are the leading estimates:

*   **Harvard Wyss Institute Estimate:** They estimate that 1 gram can hold up to 215 petabytes. In **1 mg**, this would be **215 terabytes**. For context, this is roughly equivalent to 36,000 high-definition movies stored in a speck of material the size of a grain of salt [Wyss Institute](https://wyss.harvard.edu/news/save-it-in-dna/).
*   **Maximum Theoretical Estimate:** Some researchers, such as those cited by the New Scientist, suggest a much higher theoretical limit of 455 exabytes per gram. In **1 mg**, this would equal **455 petabytes** (or 455 million gigabytes) [MedCity News](https://medcitynews.com/2015/02/one-gram-

dna-theoretically-capable-holding-data-google-facebook-combined/).
*   **The Tech Interactive Estimate:** Another estimate places the capacity at 433 exabytes per gram, which equates to **433 petabytes per 1 mg** [The Tech Interactive](https://www.thetech.org/ask-a-geneticist/articles/2024/dna-as-data/).

#### **Why DNA is a powerful storage medium:**
*   **Density:** It is millions of times more dense than conventional storage like hard drives or flash memory.
*   **Longevity:** If kept in a cool, dry environment, DNA can remain stable and readable for thousands—or even millions—of years, whereas digital media often degrades within decades.
*   **Relevance:** As long as there is life on Earth, humans will have the technology to read DNA, meaning the "format" will never truly become obsolete.

While the storage capacity is revolutionary, the main challenges currently preventing wide use are the high cost of "writing" (synthesizing) the DNA and the slow speed of "reading" (sequencing) the data back [Wyss

Institute](https://wyss.harvard.edu/news/save-it-in-dna/).
